Context

Suicide and the Church. In the play, taking one’s own life breaks God’s law, and a suicide may be refused full Christian burial (5.1.1-5, 5.1.216-219). Hamlet has already wished that God had not “fix’d / His canon ’gainst self-slaughter” (1.2.131-132). The question returns at Ophelia’s burial, where the Priest says “her death was doubtful” (5.1.215).

Critical note

Critical note: the To be, or not to be soliloquy

Critically analyse the “To be, or not to be” soliloquy.

Critical note · about 300 words

Answer. “To be, or not to be” (3.1.56-88) is Hamlet’s most famous soliloquy, a speech in which a character, alone on stage or unheard by others, speaks his thoughts aloud. It turns his private problem into a question about every human life: is it nobler to bear suffering or to end it?

The speech moves in clear stages. First Hamlet sets out the choice: to “suffer / The slings and arrows of outrageous fortune” or “to take arms against a sea of troubles” (3.1.57-59). Death looks like sleep, “a consummation / Devoutly to be wish’d” (3.1.63-64). Then the image turns against him: “To sleep: perchance to dream: ay, there’s the rub” (3.1.65). The dreams that may come after death make us stop.

Next he lists the burdens all people carry: “The pangs of despised love, the law’s delay, / The insolence of office” (3.1.72-73). Anyone could end them “With a bare bodkin” (3.1.76), a mere dagger. Only “the dread of something after death, / The undiscover’d country from whose bourn / No traveller returns” (3.1.78-80) keeps us alive.

The conclusion widens to all action: “Thus conscience does make cowards of us all” (3.1.83). Here “conscience” means both the moral sense and simple awareness. Resolution is “sicklied o’er with the pale cast of thought” (3.1.85), and great plans “lose the name of action” (3.1.88).

The technique matters. Hamlet never says “I” in the speech: he speaks of “we” and “us”, so it sounds like a general meditation, not a plan. Dramatic irony, where the audience knows more than the speaker, frames it: he believes he is alone, yet Claudius and Polonius are hidden and listening (3.1.43-55).

Verdict: the soliloquy shows the reflective mind that delays action, and it is the key evidence for any answer on Hamlet’s delay.

Short answers

Short answers

What does Claudius’s aside “How smart a lash that speech doth give my conscience!” (3.1.50) reveal?

5 marks · short answer

Answer. The aside, a remark the other characters do not hear, reveals that Claudius is guilty and that his conscience still works. It is the first time he admits a crime, though only to the audience. Polonius has just said that “with devotion’s visage / And pious action we do sugar o’er / The devil himself” (3.1.47-49). The remark strikes home. Claudius compares himself to a prostitute who covers her face with paint: “The harlot’s cheek, beautied with plastering art” (3.1.51) is no uglier than “my deed to my most painted word” (3.1.53). His fine public words are the paint on his crime. The aside confirms the Ghost’s story for the audience before the play within the play tests it, and it prepares for his prayer in 3.3.

Why is Hamlet so cruel to Ophelia in this scene?

5 marks · short answer

Answer. Hamlet’s cruelty has several causes, and a good answer weighs them. First, Ophelia has obeyed her father and rejected him; now she returns his gifts and calls him unkind: “Rich gifts wax poor when givers prove unkind” (3.1.101). Second, he may sense that they are watched. His sudden question, “Where’s your father?” (3.1.130), and her untrue reply, “At home, my lord” (3.1.131), mark the turn to fury. Third, his disgust with his mother spreads to all women: “God hath given you one face, and you make yourselves another” (3.1.143-144). The harsh “Get thee to a nunnery” (3.1.121) may also be a bitter wish to keep her from a world of “arrant knaves” (3.1.128). Ophelia is the innocent victim.

Common mistake

The mistake. Writing that Hamlet speaks “To be, or not to be” to Ophelia, or that he is planning Claudius’s murder in it.

The correction. He speaks alone, as he believes, before he sees Ophelia (3.1.88-89), and he never mentions Claudius or revenge: the speech asks whether life is worth bearing at all.

Why it costs marks. Examiners mark down an answer that misplaces a passage or misreads what it is about.

What does Ophelia’s speech “O, what a noble mind is here o’erthrown!” (3.1.150-161) show?

5 marks · short answer

Answer. Ophelia’s speech shows what Hamlet was before his grief, as she remembers him. He had the “courtier’s, soldier’s, scholar’s, eye, tongue, sword” (3.1.151): the ideal Renaissance prince, skilled in manners, arms and learning. He was “The glass of fashion and the mould of form, / The observed of all observers” (3.1.153-154), the model others copied. Now his reason sounds “Like sweet bells jangled, out of tune and harsh” (3.1.158). The speech also shows Ophelia herself: loyal, blaming no one, “of ladies most deject and wretched” (3.1.155). She believes his madness is real. Claudius, who overheard Hamlet’s words to her, decides that what Hamlet said “Was not like madness” (3.1.164).

Reference to context

Context answer

Lines 56 to 60

To be, or not to be: that is the question:
Whether ’tis nobler in the mind to suffer
The slings and arrows of outrageous fortune,
Or to take arms against a sea of troubles,
And by opposing end them? (3.1.56-60)

Reference to context · 200 to 250 words

Answer.

Reference. These lines open the most famous of Hamlet’s soliloquies, in Act 3, Scene 1 of Hamlet by William Shakespeare. Hamlet speaks, alone as he believes.

Context. Just before, Claudius and Polonius have hidden to watch Hamlet meet Ophelia, who is given a book to read so that she seems to be alone (3.1.43-55). At the end of the soliloquy, Hamlet sees her: “Soft you now! / The fair Ophelia!” (3.1.88-89).

Explanation. Hamlet asks whether it is better to live or not to live. Is it nobler to endure the blows of bad luck, or to fight a whole sea of troubles and, by fighting, end them? The images come from war: “slings and arrows” are weapons, and “take arms” means fight. To take arms against the sea is a mixed metaphor, two images that do not fit together, since no one can defeat the sea; the impossible fight hints that ending the troubles means ending the life. The question has no “I”: it is put in general terms, so it speaks for everyone. Antithesis, the balance of opposites in “To be, or not to be”, sets the pattern of the whole speech.

Critical comment. The lines turn Hamlet’s private crisis into the human question of whether life is worth its pain, and they show the thinking mind behind his delay.

(a) Gertrude

(c) Rosencrantz

(d) Ophelia

39. “The undiscover’d country from whose bourn / No traveller returns” (3.1.79-80) is:

(a) England

(b) Wittenberg

(c) death and what follows it

(d) Norway

40. Statement I: Ophelia tells Hamlet that her father is at home.
Statement II: Polonius is in fact hidden nearby, listening.

(a) Both statements are correct

(b) Both statements are incorrect

(c) Statement I is correct, Statement II is incorrect

(d) Statement I is incorrect, Statement II is correct

Keys

36. (c) alone, as he believes, while others listen in hiding. Claudius and Polonius have hidden (3.1.55); Hamlet sees Ophelia only at 3.1.88-89.

37. (a) a dagger. A bodkin is a short dagger; “bare” means mere, or unsheathed.

38. (b) Polonius. Polonius has just said that a show of devotion can “sugar o’er / The devil himself” (3.1.48-49).

39. (c) death and what follows it. The country is the world after death; “bourn” means boundary.

40. (a) Both statements are correct. “At home, my lord” (3.1.131), while Polonius and Claudius listen: “We heard it all” (3.1.180).
