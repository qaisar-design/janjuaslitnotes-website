Unit 15 · Lines 445 to 476

The Wife of Bath

A wealthy cloth-maker, five times married

A bold, deaf, gap-toothed cloth-maker from near Bath, five times wed, who has seen many great shrines and loves company.

Unit 15 · Lines 445-476 (32 lines) · Estate: the rising middle class (cloth trade) · The only laywoman among the pilgrims · Comic, double-edged · Essays 2, 9, 12

A worldly woman of huge energy: the type of the proud wife turned into a living person.

In this unit: Text · Critical note · Short answers · Context answer · Objective questions

Context

Cloth, marriage and widows. Bath lay in a cloth-making region in the west of England. The finest cloth in Europe came from Flanders, from towns such as Ypres and Ghent, so to surpass them was the highest praise. Weddings took place at the church door: the couple made their vows in the porch and then went in for Mass. In law a married woman’s goods belonged to her husband, but a widow could own property and run a trade in her own name. A woman married five times might inherit from several husbands and grow rich.

Critical note

Critical note: the Wife of Bath

Write a critical note on the Wife of Bath.

Critical note · about 300 words

Answer. The Wife of Bath is the most alive woman in the Prologue. Chaucer makes her live through a heap of solid detail that the narrator praises and the reader judges. She is the only laywoman among the pilgrims, a woman outside the Church, and she is drawn with every feature made bigger than life.

Her body tells her story. The narrator opens with praise, “A good WYF was ther of bisyde BATHE” (445), and at once adds a flaw: she is a little deaf (446). Her own Prologue, later in the Tales, explains that her fifth husband struck her on the ear. She is “Gat-tothed” (468), gap-toothed, which physiognomy, the old science of reading character from the face, took as a sign of a bold and loving nature. She has “hipes large” (472) and wears “a paire of spores sharpe” (473). Every detail can be seen and touched.

The praise carries a hidden criticism. She is “a worthy womman al hir lyve” (459), but her worth is counted in husbands. The narrator then refuses to speak of her “other companye in youthe” (461), and so makes the reader think of nothing else. Her charity fails if another wife goes first to the offering (451-452). She knows “the olde daunce” (476) of love, and experience, not innocence, is her pride.

Critics read her both as a type and as a person. Jill Mann showed that the portraits follow and transform estates satire, medieval writing that gave each class and calling its typical faults. Pride, show and a roving eye were the stock faults such writers gave to wives. Chaucer keeps them but gives her so much energy that she outgrows the type. Dryden saw the people of every age in the pilgrims: “We have our forefathers and great-grandames all before us, as they were in Chaucer’s days.”

Verdict: the Wife of Bath is the worldly wife of old satire made into a person, judged with a smile, not a sermon.

Short answers

Short answers

What do the Wife of Bath’s cloth-making and her anger at the offering show?

5 marks · short answer

Answer. They show a woman whose pride rests on her success in trade and who carries that pride into church. Cloth-making was the great industry of her region, and she is so skilled that “She passed hem of Ypres and of Gaunt” (448). Ypres and Ghent were the famous cloth towns of Flanders, so the claim is a large boast, perhaps the narrator’s exaggeration. Her trade gives her money and standing. At Mass the people of a parish went up with their offerings in order of rank. No wife in her parish dares to go before her. If one does, she grows so angry that she loses all charity (451-452). The joke is sharp. Charity, the love of one’s neighbour, is the heart of Christian worship, and she loses it during worship over a question of rank. Success in business has become pride in church.

144 words

How far does the Wife of Bath stand for a secular and a feminist position? Discuss.

5 marks · short answer

Answer. The Wife of Bath represents a secular position fully and a feminist position only in part. Secular means concerned with this world, not with the Church. Her standing comes from trade and marriage, not from faith. Feminist, in the modern sense, means claiming equal rights for women. In favour of the claim, she earns her own money as a cloth-maker and has chosen five husbands (460). She rides with spurs, and “In felawschip wel coude she laughe and carpe” (474). In a society that asked women to be silent and obedient, that is real independence. Against the claim, the word feminist belongs to a much later age. She wants to go first at the offering, not to change the order of rank. A male narrator also draws her with old beliefs about women’s appetites. She is independent, but she has no plan to change the place of women.

Common mistake

The mistake. Writing that the Wife argues, in the General Prologue, that wives should rule their husbands.

The correction. That argument comes in her own Prologue, later in the Tales. In the General Prologue she does not speak; the narrator describes her (445-476).

Why it costs marks. A question on “The Prologue” wants evidence from these lines; quoting the wrong text weakens every point.

What do the Wife of Bath’s pilgrimages tell us about her?

5 marks · short answer

Answer. Her pilgrimages show a woman of wealth, courage and appetite for the world, for whom the road matters as much as the shrine. “And thryes hadde she been at Ierusalem” (463), a long, costly and dangerous journey that few people made even once. She has also seen Rome, Boulogne, the shrine of Saint James at Compostela in Galicia, and Cologne, which claimed the bones of the Three Kings (465-466). Only a woman with money and freedom could make such journeys. The next line holds a pun, a play on two meanings: she knew much “of wandring by the weye” (467). She knows a great deal about travelling, and also about straying from the right path. Her journeys seem to offer company and pleasure more than penance, the sorrow for sin that a pilgrimage was meant to show. Canterbury is one more stop on a long road.

Reference to context

Context answer

Lines 453 to 458

Hir coverchiefs ful fyne were of ground;
I dorste swere they weyeden ten pound
That on a Sonday were upon hir heed.
Hir hosen weren of fyn scarlet reed,
Ful streite y-teyd, and shoos ful moiste and newe.
Bold was hir face, and fair, and reed of hewe. (453-458)

Reference to context · 200 to 250 words

Answer.

Reference. These lines come from the portrait of the Wife of Bath in the General Prologue to The Canterbury Tales by Geoffrey Chaucer. She is a cloth-maker from near Bath and the only laywoman among the pilgrims. The narrator speaks.

Context. Just before, we learn that she outdoes the weavers of Ypres and Ghent and grows angry if any wife goes before her to the offering (447-452). Just after, the narrator calls her “a worthy womman” (459) and counts her five husbands (460).

Explanation. Her headcloths were finely woven; the narrator would swear that those she wore on a Sunday weighed ten pounds. Her stockings were of fine scarlet, tightly tied, and her shoes were soft and new. Her face was bold, handsome and red. “coverchiefs” (453) are headcloths, “hosen” (456) are stockings, and “moiste” (457) means supple, not wet. The technique is dress as character. The narrator promised to report each pilgrim’s “array” (41), and here the clothes speak. The oath “I dorste swere” (454) introduces hyperbole, deliberate exaggeration: ten pounds of linen on one head is absurd, and the absurdity measures her vanity. Sunday, the day of worship, becomes her day for display. Scarlet was a costly cloth, and the red of her stockings returns in her face. “Bold” (458) sums up both.

Critical comment. Six lines show a woman who dresses to be seen, even in church. Kittredge’s words on the whole Tales fit her: “The Pilgrims are not static: they move and live.”

Objective questions

(a) a buckler or a targe

(b) a spade

(a) toothless

(b) gap-toothed

(c) buck-toothed

(d) gold-toothed

(a) Saint Peter

(b) Saint Thomas

(c) Saint James

(d) Saint Julian

Keys

71. (d) Flanders. “She passed hem of Ypres and of Gaunt” (448): Ypres and Ghent were the great cloth towns of Flanders.

72. (c) five. “Housbondes at chirche-dore she hadde fyve” (460).

73. (a) a buckler or a targe. “As brood as is a bokeler or a targe” (471): a buckler and a targe are round shields.

74. (b) gap-toothed. Gap teeth were read in physiognomy as a sign of a bold, loving nature. See line 468.

75. (c) Saint James. “In Galice at seint Iame, and at Coloigne” (466): the shrine of Saint James at Compostela.
