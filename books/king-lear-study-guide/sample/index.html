Context

The order of the heavens. Many critics read the storm against the Great Chain of Being, an old idea of the universe as a ladder of ranks, from the lowest kind of existence up to God, each thing in its place. In that reading, when Lear gives away his crown and his daughters turn on him, the order of nature seems to break as well. Lear calls on the gods to “Find out their enemies now” (3.2.51), but the storm does not obey a king who has given his power away.

Critical note

Critical note: the storm scene

What is the significance of the storm scene in King Lear?

Critical note · about 300 words

Answer. The storm scene (3.2) is significant because it turns Lear’s private suffering into a battle with the whole universe, and it begins both his madness and his pity. The weather is pathetic fallacy: nature that seems to share a character’s feelings.

First, the storm mirrors his mind. The Gentleman says Lear “Strives in his little world of man to out-scorn / The to-and-fro-conflicting wind and rain” (3.1.10-11). Lear then asks thunder to “Crack nature’s moulds, all germins spill at once / That make ingrateful man!” (3.2.8-9). Anger at two daughters becomes a wish to end all human life.

Second, the storm reverses his power. Once a giver of kingdoms, he cannot command the sky. He stands “your slave, / A poor, infirm, weak and despised old man” (3.2.19-20), yet calls the elements “servile ministers” (3.2.21), because they fight on his daughters’ side.

Third, it raises the question of justice. Lear asks the gods to find “undivulged crimes, / Unwhipp’d of justice” (3.2.52-53) and cries, “I am a man / More sinn’d against than sinning” (3.2.59-60). One reading hears self-pity, since Lear forgets his wrong to Cordelia; another hears the first sign that he can think of guilt beyond his own.

Fourth, madness and pity begin together. “My wits begin to turn” (3.2.67), yet at once he asks the Fool, “art cold?” (3.2.68). Hazlitt, in Characters of Shakespear’s Plays (R. Hunter and C. and J. Ollier, 1817), called the storm scene “grand and terrible” yet did not rank it among the finest; its worth lies in what it opens in Lear.

Verdict: the storm strips the king of power, shows him a man, and wakes his pity for others.

Short answers

Short answers

What does the Gentleman’s report in Act 3, Scene 1 tell us about Lear and the kingdom?

5 marks · short answer

Answer. The report shows Lear in a storm of the mind and a kingdom close to civil war. The Gentleman says Lear is “Contending with the fretful elements” (3.1.4), and that only the Fool is with him, one who “labours to out-jest / His heart-struck injuries” (3.1.16-17). The scene also gives the politics. Kent tells him that “There is division” (3.1.19) between Albany and Cornwall, and that “from France there comes a power / Into this scatter’d kingdom” (3.1.30-31). Then Kent sends the Gentleman to Dover with a ring for Cordelia (3.1.47). So the storm is both inside Lear and over the state.

What does the Fool do for Lear in the storm?

5 marks · short answer

Answer. The Fool tries to keep Lear safe, and he tells him hard truths. He begs Lear to go indoors: “court holy-water in a dry house is / better than this rain-water out o’ door” (3.2.10-11). Court holy-water means flattering words, so he advises Lear to flatter his daughters rather than drown. He jokes that “He that has a house to put’s head in has a good / head-piece” (3.2.25-26): Lear gave away his house, and his sense went with it. Lear answers with the first warmth he shows: “Poor fool and knave, I have one part in my heart / That’s sorry yet for thee” (3.2.72-73). The Fool is loyal company and the voice of common sense.

120 words

Why is Gloucester’s talk with Edmund in Act 3, Scene 3 important?

5 marks · short answer

Answer. The scene shows Gloucester’s loyalty in action and, at the same moment, hands Edmund the means to ruin him. The dukes have forbidden Gloucester to help the king, and he calls this “unnatural / dealing” (3.3.1-2). Yet he decides, “we must incline to the king” (3.3.12). Edmund, alone, plans to betray him: “This courtesy, forbid thee, shall the duke / Instantly know, and of that letter too” (3.3.19-20). He ends: “The younger rises when the old doth fall.” (3.3.23). The scene joins the two stories that run side by side, Lear’s and Gloucester’s, and the father’s kindness will cost him dearly.

Common mistake

The mistake. Writing “I am a man more sinned against than sinning”, or giving the line to Gloucester or Edgar.

The correction. Lear says it in the storm: “I am a man / More sinn’d against than sinning.” (3.2.59-60), with the spelling “sinn’d” in this text. Kent answers at once: “Alack, bare-headed!” (3.2.60).

Why it costs marks. Examiners mark down a misquoted line or a line given to the wrong speaker.

Reference to context

Context answer

Act 3, Scene 2, lines 1 to 9

Blow, winds, and crack your cheeks! rage! blow!
You cataracts and hurricanoes, spout
Till you have drench’d our steeples, drown’d the cocks!
You sulphurous and thought-executing fires,
Vaunt-couriers to oak-cleaving thunderbolts,
Singe my white head! And thou, all-shaking thunder,
Smite flat the thick rotundity o’ the world!
Crack nature’s moulds, all germins spill at once
That make ingrateful man! (3.2.1-9)

Reference to context · 200 to 250 words

Answer.

Reference. These lines open Lear’s storm speech in Act 3, Scene 2 of King Lear by William Shakespeare. Lear speaks on the heath, with the Fool beside him, in the middle of the storm.

Context. His daughters have cut down his followers and left him out in the night. In the next lines the Fool urges him to go in (3.2.10-13), but Lear refuses.

Explanation. Lear orders the wind to blow until its “cheeks” burst, the rain to drown the church steeples, and the lightning to singe his white hair. He asks the thunder to flatten the round earth and to destroy “nature’s moulds” (3.2.8), the patterns from which human beings are made, so that no more ungrateful people are born. The speech uses apostrophe, which is speech to things as if they could hear, and personification, which gives human features, such as cheeks, to the wind. Hard words such as “Crack” and “Smite” make the verse sound like the thunder it calls for.

Critical comment. Lear commands the storm like a king, yet his wish is the end of the world, not only the punishment of two daughters. The speech shows grief that has outgrown reason. Soon he sees himself as “your slave” (3.2.19), a weak old man.
