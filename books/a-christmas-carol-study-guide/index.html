The story in one page

What happens in A Christmas Carol?

A Christmas Carol is a story in five staves: on Christmas Eve a miser, Scrooge, is warned by the Ghost of his dead partner, is shown his past, his present and what may be by three Spirits, and wakes on Christmas morning to make amends. Here is the whole story stave by stave, as the book sums it up.

  1. Stave One: Marley’s Ghost Units 1 and 2

    On Christmas Eve Scrooge, a miser, sits in his counting-house, seven years after his partner Marley died (1.46). His nephew wishes him “A merry Christmas, uncle!” (1.12), and Scrooge answers “Humbug!” (1.13). He refuses two gentlemen collecting for the poor: those who would rather die had better do it and “decrease the surplus population.” (1.62). He grudges his clerk a holiday on Christmas Day (1.71 to 1.75). At home he sees Marley’s face in the door knocker (1.81). Marley’s Ghost comes through the locked door, dragging a chain of cash-boxes (1.97). It tells him “Mankind was my business.” (1.148). It promises “Three Spirits.” (1.158), the first “when the bell tolls One.” (1.163).

  2. Stave Two: the first Spirit Units 3 and 4

    At one o’clock the curtains of Scrooge’s bed are drawn aside (2.18 to 2.19). The Ghost of Christmas Past is “like a child: yet not so like a child as like an old man” (2.20). It shows Scrooge “Your past.” (2.28). He weeps for the lonely boy he was at school (2.54). His little sister Fan comes to take the boy home (2.67 to 2.70). Old Fezziwig, the master of his youth, gives a Christmas Eve ball (2.93). Then Belle, the girl he was to marry, releases him: “Another idol has displaced me” (2.112). He sees her happy family years later (2.136). At last he presses the Ghost’s cap down over its light (2.149), and sleeps.

  3. Stave Three: the second Spirit Units 5 and 6

    The Ghost of Christmas Present is “a jolly Giant, glorious to see” (3.5). It leads Scrooge through the Christmas streets to the house of his clerk, Bob Cratchit (3.41). The family enjoy their goose and pudding, and Tiny Tim says “God bless us every one!” (3.68). The Ghost warns that, if nothing changes, “the child will die.” (3.71). Then it repeats Scrooge’s own words about the “surplus population.” (3.73). They visit miners (3.90), a lighthouse (3.93) and a ship (3.95). At the party of Scrooge’s nephew, Fred, the guests drink to “Uncle Scrooge!” (3.127). As its life ends, the Ghost shows two wretched children: “This boy is Ignorance. This girl is Want.” (3.144). Then “a solemn Phantom, draped and hooded” (3.148) comes towards Scrooge.

  4. Stave Four: the last Spirit Units 7 and 8

    The Ghost of Christmas Yet To Come “neither spoke nor moved.” (4.3). It shows Scrooge what may be. Rich men joke that a dead man will have “a very cheap funeral” (4.24). In old Joe’s shop a charwoman, a laundress and an undertaker’s man sell the dead man’s things (4.44). His body lies alone in the dark, and Scrooge cannot uncover the face (4.80). A young couple who owed him money are glad: “it was a happier house for this man’s death!” (4.103). The Cratchits mourn Tiny Tim, and Bob cries “My little, little child!” (4.122). In a churchyard Scrooge reads on a grave “his own name, EBENEZER SCROOGE.” (4.155). He promises: “I will honour Christmas in my heart, and try to keep it all the year.” (4.164).

  5. Stave Five: the end of it Units 9 and 10

    Scrooge wakes in his own bed, with time “to make amends in!” (5.1). A boy in the street tells him that it is Christmas Day (5.16). Scrooge decides that “The Spirits have done it all in one night.” (5.17). He sends the prize Turkey to Bob Cratchit in secret: “He sha’n’t know who sends it.” (5.29). He promises the portly gentleman a large gift for the poor, with “A great many back-payments” (5.42). He dines with his nephew: “Will you let me in, Fred?” (5.58). Next morning he catches Bob coming late, and says “I am about to raise your salary!” (5.67). The narrator tells us that Tiny Tim “did NOT die” (5.70). The story ends with Tiny Tim’s words: “God bless Us, Every One!” (5.71).

Dickens wrote A Christmas Carol in the autumn of 1843, in about six weeks. Chapman and Hall published it on 19 December 1843, at five shillings, and the first printing of 6,000 copies had sold out by Christmas Eve. The story has five staves and 709 numbered paragraphs.

A Christmas Carol stave by stave: the 10 units

The book gives two units to each stave: 10 units in all, from Scrooge in his counting-house to Scrooge changed. Each line below is the book’s own summary of the unit.

  1. 1

    Unit 1: Marley Was Dead: Scrooge in His Counting-house Stave One, paragraphs 1 to 78

    On Christmas Eve, Scrooge turns away his nephew, refuses the two gentlemen collecting for the poor, and grumbles at his clerk’s holiday.

  2. 2

    Unit 2: Marley’s Ghost: “I Wear the Chain I Forged in Life” Stave One, paragraphs 79 to 173

    Scrooge sees Marley’s face in the knocker. Then Marley’s Ghost, dragging a chain of cash-boxes, warns him that three Spirits will come.

  3. 3

    Unit 3: The Ghost of Christmas Past: Scrooge’s Lonely Boyhood Stave Two, paragraphs 1 to 79

    The first Spirit takes Scrooge back to his childhood. He weeps for his lonely self at school, then meets his little sister.

  4. 4

    Unit 4: Fezziwig and Belle: “Another Idol Has Displaced Me” Stave Two, paragraphs 80 to 151

    The Ghost shows Scrooge his old master’s Christmas ball. Then the girl he loved sets him free because he loves money more.

  5. 5

    Unit 5: The Ghost of Christmas Present and the Cratchits’ Christmas Stave Three, paragraphs 1 to 85

    The second Spirit takes Scrooge to Bob Cratchit’s poor home. The family enjoy their dinner; Scrooge asks if Tiny Tim will live.

  6. 6

    Unit 6: Christmas Everywhere: From the Miners to Ignorance and Want Stave Three, paragraphs 86 to 148

    The Ghost shows Scrooge miners, lighthouse keepers and sailors keeping Christmas, then his nephew’s merry party, and at last two starving children.

  7. 7

    Unit 7: The Last of the Spirits: A Death Without a Mourner Stave Four, paragraphs 1 to 84

    A silent Phantom shows Scrooge what is to come. Rich men joke about a man’s death, thieves sell his things, and his body lies alone.

  8. 8

    Unit 8: The Vision of Tiny Tim’s Death and the Churchyard Stave Four, paragraphs 85 to 166

    Scrooge sees a family in debt relieved by the man’s death, the Cratchits mourning Tiny Tim, and at last his own grave.

  9. 9

    Unit 9: The End of It: Christmas Morning Stave Five, paragraphs 1 to 35

    Scrooge wakes in his own bed with time to make amends. He laughs and cries at once, learns it is Christmas Day, and sends the prize Turkey to Bob Cratchit.

  10. 10

    Unit 10: Scrooge Changed: “As Good a Friend, as Good a Master” Stave Five, paragraphs 36 to 71

    Scrooge walks out in his best clothes, promises a large gift to the poor, dines at Fred’s, and next morning raises Bob Cratchit’s salary.

The characters

A Christmas Carol character analysis: who is who in the story?

A Christmas Carol turns on one man and the Ghosts who visit him, with the Cratchits, Fezziwig and Ignorance and Want to show him what he has missed. The book has ten drawings in one house style; here they are with the book’s own notes on each.

Ebenezer Scrooge

A London man of business, “a squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous, old sinner!” (1.6). The Spirits change him into “as good a man, as the good old city knew” (5.70).

Jacob Marley’s Ghost

Scrooge’s partner, dead “these seven years” (1.46). His Ghost wears a chain of cash-boxes and warns Scrooge that three Spirits will come.

The Ghost of Christmas Past

The first Spirit, with “a bright clear jet of light” (2.20) springing from its head. It shows Scrooge his boyhood, Fezziwig and Belle.

Old Fezziwig

Scrooge’s old master, “an old gentleman in a Welsh wig” (2.83). He gives his apprentices and workers a Christmas Eve ball.

The Ghost of Christmas Present

The second Spirit, a giant who carries “a glowing torch” (3.5). It shows Scrooge the Cratchits, Fred’s party, and Ignorance and Want.

Ignorance and Want

Two wretched children who come from the robe of the Ghost of Christmas Present: “They are Man’s” (3.144).

The Ghost of Christmas Yet To Come

The last Spirit, a silent Phantom “shrouded in a deep black garment” (4.2). It points to a lonely death and a neglected grave.

Bob Cratchit

Scrooge’s clerk, on “fifteen shillings a week” (1.43); the text first names him in Stave Three (3.41). He is gentle, loyal and a loving father.

Scrooge on Christmas morning

Scrooge wakes in his own bed with time to make amends. He laughs and cries at once, learns it is Christmas Day, and sends the prize Turkey to Bob Cratchit.

Scrooge changed

Scrooge walks out in his best clothes, promises a large gift to the poor, dines at Fred’s, and next morning raises Bob Cratchit’s salary.

Questions and answers

A Christmas Carol questions students ask

Short, direct answers taken from the A Christmas Carol study guide itself.

Does this book include the full text of A Christmas Carol?

Yes. It prints the whole story, all five staves, from the first edition (Chapman and Hall, London, 1843), in 10 units, two for each stave. Every one of the 709 numbered paragraphs has a plain modern English paragraph after it, and 517 hard words and phrases are explained in word notes after the passages.

Is there a modern English version of every paragraph of A Christmas Carol?

Yes. Every paragraph of Dickens is followed by its meaning in plain modern English: a short paragraph has its modern English directly beneath it, and a long one is followed by its modern English as one paragraph. “Bah!” said Scrooge, “Humbug!” (1.13), for example, becomes “Bah!” said Scrooge. “A fraud!”

What are the most important themes in A Christmas Carol?

The book follows six themes through the story: money (money and greed), change (Scrooge’s change and the power of memory), poverty (poverty, the poor and their children), family (family and home), Christmas (Christmas and the Christmas spirit) and ghosts (the four Ghosts and their warnings). Every point on a unit card carries its theme tag, and each tag leads to the essays that need it; poverty, for example, leads to Essay 3, on Dickens’s social message, and Essay 9, on the children in the story.

Which A Christmas Carol essay questions come up most often?

The book’s twelve model essays answer the A Christmas Carol questions examiners set most often: Scrooge’s character and change, the four Ghosts, poverty and the poor, Christmas, family, Marley’s Ghost and the chain, the structure of the five staves, the narrator’s voice and humour, Tiny Tim, Ignorance and Want, money and greed, memory and time, and symbols and imagery. The book marks four of them “Frequently set”: Scrooge’s character and change, the four Ghosts, poverty and the poor, and family. Each essay is written in full for long papers, with a marked cut line where a shorter answer can stop.

Who are the Ghosts in A Christmas Carol?

Four Ghosts visit Scrooge. The first is Marley’s Ghost, in Stave One. The other three are the Spirits: the Ghost of Christmas Past in Stave Two, the Ghost of Christmas Present in Stave Three and the Ghost of Christmas Yet To Come in Stave Four. So Marley’s Ghost is a Ghost, but he is not one of the three Spirits.

Why is A Christmas Carol divided into staves?

Because Dickens calls the book a carol, and a stave is a verse of a song. The five staves are Marley’s Ghost, The First of the Three Spirits, The Second of the Three Spirits, The Last of the Spirits and The End of It. This book gives each stave two units, and numbers every paragraph of the story, so a reference such as (1.6) means Stave One, paragraph 6.

Do the Spirits visit Scrooge in one night or three?

The text does not settle it. Marley’s Ghost promises one Spirit a night, on three nights (1.163 to 1.165), but in Stave Five Scrooge says “The Spirits have done it all in one night.” (5.17).

Will the paragraph numbers match my copy of A Christmas Carol?

Not always. The book numbers every paragraph of the 1843 text, and every speech counts as one paragraph, however short, so the numbers will not match the pages of your own copy. Find the number in the margin of the book’s text. Examiners name passages by stave and by their sense, so know each passage by what it says as well as by its number.
