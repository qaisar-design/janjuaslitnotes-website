The poem in one page

What happens in Paradise Lost, Book I?

Book I of Paradise Lost tells its story in six movements: the invocation, the burning lake, the plain and the call to the host, the roll of the fallen angels, the host in array, and Pandemonium and the council. Here is Book I in brief, as the book sums it up.

  1. The invocation Unit 1 · Lines 1 to 26

    The narrator, the voice that tells the story, states his subject: “Of Man’s first disobedience” (1). He prays to the Heavenly Muse and to the Spirit for help. His aim is to “justify the ways of God to men” (26).

  2. The burning lake Units 2 to 5 · Lines 27 to 241

    The narrator asks what first caused the Fall and answers: “Th’ infernal Serpent” (34). Thrown out of Heaven, the rebel and his crew lie on a burning lake for “Nine times the space that measures day and night” (50). Hell has fire but “No light; but rather darkness visible” (63). Satan wakes and speaks to Beelzebub, his next in command. He refuses to repent: “All is not lost” (106). Beelzebub fears that God is almighty. Satan answers with a plan to turn God’s good into evil. He rises from the lake, huge as the sea-beast Leviathan, and flies to the burning land. Heaven allows it: God “Left him at large to his own dark designs” (213).

  3. The plain and the call Units 6 to 8 · Lines 242 to 375

    On the plain Satan greets Hell as its “new possessor” (252). He declares: “Better to reign in Hell than serve in Heaven.” (263). Beelzebub says his voice will wake the host, the army of fallen angels. Satan walks to the shore with his huge shield and spear. His followers lie stunned on the lake, thick as autumn leaves. He calls: “Awake, arise, or be for ever fallen!” (330). They rise like a cloud of locusts and gather on the plain.

  4. The roll of the fallen Units 9 to 11 · Lines 376 to 521

    The narrator calls on the Muse again: “Say, Muse, their names then known” (376). He names the chiefs by the names of the false gods they later became among men. Moloch comes first and Belial last of the principal chiefs; the Ionian gods follow.

  5. The host in array Units 12 to 14 · Lines 522 to 669

    Satan lifts the host’s courage, and Azazel raises the standard, Satan’s great flag. The army forms up in silence. Satan stands above it, still bright yet scarred, no less than “Archangel ruined” (593). He weeps, then speaks. He plans a new war “by fraud or guile” (646), against a new world that God may create. “Millions of flaming swords” (664) flash in reply.

  6. Pandemonium and the council Units 15 to 17 · Lines 670 to 798

    Mammon leads a band to a hill full of ore, and they cast gold. A palace rises to music: Pandemonium, built by Mulciber. Heralds call a council. The host swarms into the hall like bees and shrinks to a tiny size, while the chiefs keep their own. The book ends as the council begins: “the great consult began” (798).

Milton was blind when he composed Paradise Lost and dictated it. The poem was published in ten books in late 1667, and the second edition of 1674, in twelve books, is the text this book uses. Book I has 798 lines of blank verse, unrhymed lines of ten syllables, and it ends as the council begins.

Questions and answers

Paradise Lost questions students ask

Short, direct answers taken from the Paradise Lost study guide itself.

Does this book include the whole of Paradise Lost?

No. It is Paradise Lost, Book I, the first book of Milton’s epic, complete: all 798 lines, from “Of Man’s first disobedience” (1) to “the great consult began” (798), in 17 units. The text is a modern-spelling text of the second edition (S. Simmons, London, 1674), with obvious misprints corrected. Under every line there is a plain modern English line, with word notes after each passage.

Is there a modern English translation of every line of Paradise Lost, Book I?

Yes. Every line of Book I has a plain modern English line beneath it, and word notes explain the hard words and names. “Better to reign in Hell than serve in Heaven.” (263), for example, becomes “It is better to rule in Hell than to serve in Heaven.”

What are the most important themes in Paradise Lost, Book I?

The book follows six themes through Book I: pride (pride and ambition), will (the will that refuses to yield), Hell (Hell as a place and a state of mind), epic (epic form, the epic simile and the grand style), power (power, leadership and God’s rule) and the Fall (disobedience, fall and loss). Every point on a unit card carries its theme tag, and each tag leads to the essays that need it; pride, for example, leads to Essays 1 and 3.

Which Paradise Lost, Book I essay questions come up most often?

The book has twelve model essays and marks five of them “Frequently set”: Satan in Book I, epic conventions and the epic simile, the subject and themes, whether Satan is the hero, and the invocation. The other seven are on Hell, the fallen angels, Beelzebub and Satan’s leadership, Milton’s grand style and blank verse, “justify the ways of God to men”, Pandemonium and Mammon, and Milton and his age. Each essay is written in full for long papers, with a marked cut line where a shorter answer can stop.

Is Satan the hero of Paradise Lost, Book I?

The book treats it as an open debate and gives both sides. Satan has a hero’s courage, energy and eloquence, and the poem wins him sympathy; yet the narrator judges him at every turn, and God’s plan frames all his actions. The book’s own answer, offered as one view, is that Satan is the hero of Book I in form but not in meaning, and a strong exam answer weighs both sides before it judges.

Who speaks in Paradise Lost, Book I?

Only two characters speak. Satan has five speeches (lines 84-124, 157-191, 242-270, 315-330 and 622-662) and Beelzebub has two (128-155 and 272-282). Every other line is the narrator’s, the voice that tells the story, including the invocation. Moloch, Belial and Mammon are named and described, but they do not speak in Book I.

What does “justify the ways of God to men” mean?

It means to show people that God’s dealings with them are just. The book explains that “justify” here means to declare the justice of God’s ways, not to make excuses for him. The phrase pairs with “I may assert Eternal Providence” (25), and it sets a hard task: the poem must explain why a good God allowed the first disobedience and the misery that followed. It is the last line of the invocation (26).

Will the line numbers match my edition of Paradise Lost?

Not always. The line numbers are this book’s own, and other editions may differ by a line or two. Book I has 798 lines, and the key lines stand at their usual numbers: “Better to reign in Hell than serve in Heaven.” is line 263 and “Awake, arise, or be for ever fallen!” is line 330. Find the passage by its words first, then check the number beside it.
