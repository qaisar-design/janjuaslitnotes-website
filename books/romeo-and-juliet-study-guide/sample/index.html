Context

The balcony that is not there. Everyone calls Act 2, Scene 2 the balcony scene, but the word “balcony” is not in the play. The stage direction says only, “Juliet appears above at a window.” Romeo stands below in Capulet’s orchard, having climbed its high walls. Juliet warns him that the place is “death, considering who thou art,” (2.2.64) if her kinsmen find him. The height between them pictures the distance their families have set between them, and the night that hides them is both their protection and their danger.

Critical note

Critical note: the balcony scene

What is the significance of the balcony scene in Romeo and Juliet?

Critical note · about 300 words

Answer. The balcony scene (2.2) turns a meeting into a promise of marriage. It shows the power of the lovers’ feeling, Juliet’s honesty and courage, and the danger and haste that will destroy them.

First, it shows love through light. Romeo sees Juliet as the dawn: “It is the east, and Juliet is the sun!” (2.2.3). She is a “bright angel” (2.2.26) above him. Even in darkness, she is the light of his world.

Second, Juliet questions the feud. Not knowing that Romeo hears, she says the quarrel is only a word: “’Tis but thy name that is my enemy;” (2.2.38). A rose would smell as sweet by any other name, and Romeo would be as dear by another.

Third, Juliet is frank and practical. Once overheard, she drops pretence: “but farewell compliment!” (2.2.89). She asks him directly, “Dost thou love me?” (2.2.90), and it is she who proposes marriage: “send me word to-morrow,” (2.2.144).

Fourth, the scene is shadowed by danger and speed. Juliet warns that her kinsmen “will murder thee” (2.2.70), and Romeo would rather die than live without her love (2.2.77-78). Juliet fears that the vow is “Too like the lightning” (2.2.119), gone in a moment, which is a warning that the play fulfils.

William Hazlitt, in Characters of Shakespear’s Plays (R. Hunter and C. and J. Ollier, 1817), wrote, “It would be hard to say which of the two garden scenes is the finest”, meaning this scene and the parting at dawn in Act 3, Scene 5. The two scenes frame the marriage: here it is promised, there it ends in separation.

Verdict: the scene is the play’s high point of joy, yet every image of light and every promise is touched by the danger of the night.

Short answers

Short answers

How does Mercutio mock Romeo in Act 2, Scene 1?

5 marks · short answer

Answer. Mercutio mocks Romeo by pretending to raise him like a spirit, with crude jokes about love. He calls him “Romeo! humours! madman! passion! lover!” (2.1.7) and asks for one sigh or rhyme. Then he conjures him by Rosaline’s body, from “her scarlet lip” (2.1.18) to “quivering thigh,” (2.1.19). Benvolio says love is blind and fits the dark, and Mercutio twists the idea with a bawdy joke: “If love be blind, love cannot hit the mark.” (2.1.33). He does not know that Romeo has already forgotten Rosaline. The scene ends with Romeo’s reply, which opens Act 2, Scene 2: “He jests at scars that never felt a wound.” (2.2.1). Mercutio’s mockery sets off the seriousness of Romeo’s new love.

128 words

What does Juliet’s “wherefore art thou Romeo?” speech show about her?

5 marks · short answer

Answer. The speech shows that Juliet thinks clearly and boldly about love and the feud. “O Romeo, Romeo! wherefore art thou Romeo?” (2.2.33) means “why are you Romeo?”, a Montague. She asks him to give up his name, or she will give up hers. She reasons that a name is not part of a person: “What’s in a name? that which we call a rose / By any other name would smell as sweet;” (2.2.43-44). Then she offers herself completely: “Take all myself.” (2.2.49). The speech shows a girl who will put love before family, and who sees the feud as empty words. It also shows her innocence, because a name can kill in Verona.

119 words

How does the Prologue to Act 2 sum up the lovers’ situation?

5 marks · short answer

Answer. The second Prologue, again a sonnet, says that the old love has died and the new one faces danger. Romeo’s love for Rosaline is gone: “Now old desire doth in his death-bed lie,” (2.0.1). He and Juliet are “Alike bewitched by the charm of looks,” (2.0.6), both won by sight. But the feud is a barrier: “Being held a foe, he may not have access” (2.0.9), and Juliet has even fewer chances to meet him. The sonnet ends with hope: “But passion lends them power, time means, to meet,” (2.0.13). It stresses both the strength of their love and the danger around it, and it explains Romeo’s climb into the orchard that follows.

Common mistake

The mistake. Translating “wherefore art thou Romeo?” as “where are you, Romeo?”

The correction. “Wherefore” means “why”. Juliet does not know that Romeo is below. She asks why he must be Romeo, a Montague: “O Romeo, Romeo! wherefore art thou Romeo? / Deny thy father and refuse thy name;” (2.2.33-34).

Why it costs marks. The error turns a speech about the feud into a search for a missing person, and it shows the examiner that the speech has not been understood.

Reference to context

Context answer

Act 2, Scene 2, lines 116 to 124

Well, do not swear: although I joy in thee,
I have no joy of this contract to-night:
It is too rash, too unadvised, too sudden,
Too like the lightning, which doth cease to be
Ere one can say ‘It lightens.’ Sweet, good night!
This bud of love, by summer’s ripening breath,
May prove a beauteous flower when next we meet.
Good night, good night! as sweet repose and rest
Come to thy heart as that within my breast! (2.2.116-124)

Reference to context · 200 to 250 words

Answer.

Reference. These lines are spoken by Juliet in Act 2, Scene 2 of Romeo and Juliet by William Shakespeare. She is at her window above Capulet’s orchard, and Romeo stands below.

Context. Romeo has overheard Juliet confess her love. He begins to swear by the moon, and she stops him, because the moon is “inconstant” (2.2.109). He starts again, and she interrupts.

Explanation. Juliet tells Romeo not to swear. Although he gives her joy, this exchange of promises tonight worries her. It is too hasty, too thoughtless, too sudden, like lightning, which is gone before anyone can say that it has flashed. She hopes that their love, now only a bud, will grow into a beautiful flower by the time they next meet. She says good night and wishes him the same peace that she feels.

Critical comment. The lightning simile, a comparison using “like”, is the key to the play’s speed: a flash of brightness that is quickly gone. The repetition of “too” shows Juliet’s good sense even in love. Yet within a few lines she proposes marriage. The bud and flower image prepares for later images of flowers killed by frost, when her father finds her seemingly dead.
