In this unit: Text · Critical note · Short answers · Context answer · Objective questions

Context

A sacrifice or a murder? Othello enters believing that he is an instrument of justice, not a murderer: Desdemona “must die, else she’ll betray more men.” (5.2.6). He wants her to pray first so that he will not kill her soul, as a Christian executioner might. Desdemona calls his act what it is: “That death’s unnatural that kills for loving.” (5.2.44). When Othello says her lie has made him “call what I intend to do / A murder, which I thought a sacrifice:” (5.2.66-67), the whole moral question of the scene is in one sentence.

Critical note

Critical note: the murder scene

Discuss Othello’s soliloquy at the start of Act 5, Scene 2. (Frequently set)

Critical note · about 300 words

Answer. Othello’s soliloquy over the sleeping Desdemona is one of the most beautiful and terrible speeches in Shakespeare. It shows a man who still loves deeply, who believes he is doing justice, and who is about to commit murder.

First, he sees himself as a priest or judge. He begins, “It is the cause, it is the cause, my soul:” (5.2.1), refusing to name her supposed crime to the “chaste stars!” (5.2.2). He will not shed her blood or scar her skin, “whiter skin of hers than snow / And smooth as monumental alabaster.” (5.2.4-5). The image of a tomb statue makes her already seem dead.

Second, he states his reason as justice: “Yet she must die, else she’ll betray more men.” (5.2.6). He believes he is protecting others.

Third, the central image is light. “Put out the light, and then put out the light:” (5.2.7) means first the candle, then her life. A candle can be lit again, but he knows no “Promethean heat” (5.2.12) can relight her. The image of the rose follows: once plucked, it cannot grow again, “I’ll smell it on the tree.” (5.2.15).

Fourth, love fights with resolve. He kisses her again and again: “One more, one more:” (5.2.17). He weeps: “this sorrow’s heavenly; / It strikes where it doth love.” (5.2.21-22). He compares himself to God, who punishes those he loves.

The speech restores the music of Othello’s early verse, so the audience feels the greatness of what is being destroyed. Yet his calm is a terrible self-deception. He calls murder a sacrifice and his jealousy justice.

Verdict: the soliloquy shows Othello at his most loving and most deluded, and it makes the murder a tragedy rather than a crime alone.

Short answers

Short answers

How does Desdemona plead for her life?

5 marks · short answer

Answer. Desdemona pleads with innocence, then with desperation. She denies giving Cassio the handkerchief: “No, by my life and soul! / Send for the man, and ask him.” (5.2.51-52). She says her only sins are “loves I bear to you.” (5.2.42). When Othello says Cassio is dead, she weeps, “Alas, he is betray’d, and I undone!” (5.2.79), which he takes as guilt. Then she begs: “O, banish me, my lord, but kill me not!” (5.2.81), “Kill me to-morrow; let me live to-night!” (5.2.83), “But half an hour!” (5.2.85), and “But while I say one prayer!” (5.2.86). Each plea asks for less. Othello answers, “It is too late.” (5.2.86), and stifles her.

Why does Desdemona say “Nobody; I myself.”?

5 marks · short answer

Answer. Desdemona revives for a moment and tries to protect Othello. She cries, “O, falsely, falsely murder’d!” (5.2.120) and “A guiltless death I die.” (5.2.125). When Emilia asks, “O, who hath done this deed?” (5.2.126), she answers, “Nobody; I myself. Farewell: / Commend me to my kind lord:” (5.2.127-128). Her last words are a lie told out of love: she forgives her murderer and calls him “kind”. The moment shows her goodness at its highest. It also exposes Othello, who at first uses her words to deny the deed, then admits, “’Twas I that kill’d her.” (5.2.133). Her loyalty to death answers every charge Iago made.

114 words

How does Emilia respond when she learns the truth?

5 marks · short answer

Answer. Emilia responds with grief, fury and courage. When Othello confesses, she answers, “O, the more angel she, / And you the blacker devil!” (5.2.133-134). Learning that Iago accused her mistress, she repeats in horror, “My husband!” (5.2.143). She sees the truth at once: “villany hath made mocks with love!” (5.2.154). She curses Iago: “may his pernicious soul / Rot half a grain a day!” (5.2.158-159). She defies Othello’s sword: “Thou hast not half that power to do me harm / As I have to be hurt.” (5.2.165-166). Then she cries out to the house: “The Moor hath kill’d my mistress! Murder! murder!” (5.2.170). The obedient wife becomes the voice of truth.

Common mistake

The mistake. Writing that Othello stabs or poisons Desdemona.

The correction. He stifles her in her bed (the direction at 5.2.86, “He stifles her.”); the Folio says “Smothers her.” Iago had urged him to “strangle her in her bed,” (4.1.201).

Why it costs marks. The manner of death keeps his promise not to “shed her blood,” (5.2.3) and should be stated exactly.

Reference to context

Context answer

Act 5, Scene 2, lines 1 to 15

It is the cause, it is the cause, my soul:
Let me not name it to you, you chaste stars!
It is the cause. Yet I’ll not shed her blood,
Nor scar that whiter skin of hers than snow
And smooth as monumental alabaster.
Yet she must die, else she’ll betray more men.
Put out the light, and then put out the light:
If I quench thee, thou flaming minister,
I can again thy former light restore,
Should I repent me: but once put out thy light,
Thou cunning’st pattern of excelling nature,
I know not where is that Promethean heat
That can thy light relume. When I have pluck’d the rose,
I cannot give it vital growth again,
It must needs wither: I’ll smell it on the tree. (5.2.1-15)

Reference to context · 200 to 250 words

Answer.

Reference. These lines are spoken by Othello, alone with the sleeping Desdemona, at the start of Act 5, Scene 2 of Othello by William Shakespeare, in the bedchamber in the castle.

Context. Othello has heard Cassio’s cries and believes Iago has killed him. He has come to kill Desdemona.

Explanation. Othello says that “the cause”, her supposed crime, drives him, but he will not name it to the pure stars. He will not shed her blood or scar her skin, whiter than snow and smooth as the alabaster of a tomb. Yet she must die, or she will betray more men. He will put out the candle, then her life. A candle can be relit if he regrets it, but once her light is out, no fire like the one Prometheus stole from the gods can relight it. A plucked rose cannot grow again; he will smell it on the tree.

Critical comment. The speech is calm, slow and beautiful, in strong contrast with Othello’s broken prose in Act 4. Its images of whiteness, light and the rose stress Desdemona’s purity, which is the opposite of what he believes. The repeated “the cause” shows him persuading himself that murder is justice. The dramatic irony is total: he speaks of her betraying “more men” when she has betrayed none.

Keys

66. (b) He will put out the candle and then end Desdemona’s life. The first “light” is the candle, “thou flaming minister,” (5.2.8); the second is Desdemona’s life, which cannot be relit (5.2.10-13).

67. (a) A-2, B-1, C-3, D-2. Desdemona (5.2.44), Othello (5.2.86), Emilia (5.2.133-134), Desdemona (5.2.127).
